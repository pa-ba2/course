Business Analytics-2 Final Project Breakdown:
This final project is your opportunity to apply the business analytics concepts and
techniques you've learned throughout this course to a real-world scenario. Working
through the following steps demonstrates your ability to identify a business question,
analyze relevant data, and communicate actionable insights.

1. Project Title: Giving Your Analysis a Name
What it is: Your project needs a clear and concise title that reflects the core topic or
question you are investigating. Think of it as the headline for your data story.

Why it's important: A good title helps you and others quickly understand the focus of
your project. It sets the stage for your analysis and makes your work more organized.

How to create a good title:
  ●​ Be specific: Avoid vague titles. Instead of "Sales Analysis," consider "Analyzing
  Factors Influencing Online Sales Growth in Q3 2025."
  ●​ Reflect the core question: If your project aims to answer a specific question, try
  to incorporate it into the title. For example, "Can Social Media Engagement
  Predict Website Conversions?"
  ●​ Keep it concise: Aim for a title that is informative but not too long or overly
  complex.
  ●​ Consider the business context: If your project is based on a specific business
  or industry, you should include that.

Example Titles:
  ●​ Analyzing Customer Churn in the Telecommunications Industry
  ●​ Predicting Housing Prices Based on Location and Features
  ●​ The Impact of Marketing Campaigns on Product Sales
  ●​ Optimizing Inventory Management for a Retail Business
  ●​ Understanding Factors Driving Employee Satisfaction

2. Goals & Expected Outcomes: Setting Your Objectives
What it is: This section clearly outlines what you aim to achieve with your project
(Goals) and what tangible results or insights you expect to produce (Outcomes).
  ●​ Goals: These are the broader objectives you want to accomplish through your
  analysis. They should be aligned with the project title and the business context.

---

  ●​ Expected Outcomes: These are the specific deliverables or insights you
  anticipate generating by the end of your project. They should be measurable and
  directly relate to your goals.

How to define your Goals & Outcomes:
  ●​ Start with "To...": Frame your goals using action verbs starting with "To," such
  as "To understand...", "To identify...", "To predict...", "To evaluate...".
  ●​ Be specific and measurable (as much as possible for this stage): While you
  might not have concrete numbers yet, try to be as specific as possible about
  what you want to achieve.
  ●​ Think about the "so what?": Why is this analysis important? What business
  value will it provide?
  ●​ Consider different types of outcomes: These could include:
  ○​ Identification of key trends or patterns in the data.
  ○​ Development of predictive models.
  ○​ Evaluation of the effectiveness of a particular strategy.
  ○​ Recommendations for business improvement.
  ○​ Visualizations that clearly communicate key insights.

Example Goals & Outcomes:

Project Title: Analyzing Customer Churn in the Telecommunications Industry
  ●​ Goals:
  ○​ To identify the key factors that contribute to customer churn in the
  telecommunications industry.
  ○​ To understand the characteristics of customers who are likely to churn.
  ○​ To provide insights that can inform strategies for customer retention.
  ●​ Expected Outcomes:
  ○​ A clear list of the top 3-5 factors associated with customer churn.
  ○​ Visualizations showing the differences between churning and
  non-churning customers across key variables.
  ○​ A profile of the "at-risk" customer segment.
  ○​ Potential recommendations for targeted retention efforts.

3. S.W.O.T. Analysis: Examining the Project Landscape
What it is: SWOT analysis is a strategic planning tool used to evaluate the Strengths,
Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats related to your project. It helps you think
critically about the internal and external factors that could impact your success.

---

Why it's important for your project:
  ●​ Self-Reflection: It encourages you to think about what you can do well with the
  skills and resources you have (Strengths) and where you might face challenges
  (Weaknesses).
  ●​ External Awareness: It prompts you to consider potential positive external
  factors you can leverage (Opportunities) and negative external factors you need
  to be aware of (Threats).
  ●​ Planning and Mitigation: By identifying these factors, you can better plan your
  project, capitalize on opportunities, and develop strategies to mitigate
  weaknesses and threats.

How to conduct a SWOT Analysis for your project:

Think about your project in the context of your current skills, the available data, and the
overall learning environment. Consider these questions for each element:
  ●​ Strengths (Internal, Positive):
  ○​ What business analytics skills have you developed that are relevant to this
  project?
  ○​ What tools are you comfortable using?
  ○​ What access do you have to relevant data or resources?
  ○​ What are you particularly interested in or motivated to learn more about?
  ●​ Weaknesses (Internal, Negative):
  ○​ What areas of business analytics are you still developing your skills in?
  ○​ Are there any software tools you are less familiar with?
  ○​ Do you anticipate any challenges in accessing or cleaning the data?
  ○​ What are your time management skills like for a project of this scope?
  ●​ Opportunities (External, Positive):
  ○​ Are there interesting datasets publicly available that align with your
  interests?
  ○​ Can you learn new software tools or techniques as part of this project?
  ○​ Is there a real-world business scenario you can explore (even
  hypothetically)?
  ○​ Can you collaborate with classmates to learn from each other?
  ●​ Threats (External, Negative):
  ○​ Could the available data be incomplete or unreliable?
  ○​ Are there time constraints that might impact your ability to complete the
  project?
  ○​ Could there be technical difficulties with the software tools?
  ○​ Are there other commitments that might distract you from the project?

---

Example of a SWOT Analysis for a Project:

Let's say a student is doing a project on "Analyzing customer satisfaction trends for a
local e-commerce business."
  ●​ Strengths:
  ○​ Proficient in data visualization with Tableau.
  ○​ Good understanding of customer segmentation concepts.
  ○​ Motivated to improve data analysis skills.
  ●​ Weaknesses:
  ○​ Limited experience with data cleaning using Python.
  ○​ Basic knowledge of statistical analysis.
  ○​ Time constraints due to other coursework.
  ●​ Opportunities:
  ○​ Access to the e-commerce business's customer review data.
  ○​ Opportunity to learn Python for data manipulation.
  ○​ Potential to present findings to the local business for real-world impact.
  ●​ Threats:
  ○​ Incomplete or biased customer review data.
  ○​ Tight project deadline.
  ○​ Potential technical issues with data analysis software.

Present your SWOT analysis in a clear, bulleted list for each of the four
categories.

5. README File: Your Project's User Manual
What it is: A README file is a standard document in the world of software
development and data science. It provides essential information about your project to
anyone who wants to understand or reproduce your work. Think of it as a guide that
accompanies your project files.

Why it's important for your project:
  ●​ Clarity and Documentation: It clearly explains what your project is about, how
  it's organized, and how to run or understand your analysis.
  ●​ Reproducibility: It provides the necessary steps and information for someone
  else (or your future self) to replicate your work.
  ●​ Organization: It helps you structure your project files logically and explain that
  structure to others.
  ●​ Communication: It serves as a primary point of contact for understanding your
  project.

How to create a good README.md file (using Markdown format):

A README file is typically written in Markdown (.md) format, which is a simple way to
add formatting to plain text. Here's a template and explanation of what to include:

# Customer Churn Analysis in Telecommunications

## Project Overview

**Brief Description:**
This project analyzes customer data from the telecommunications industry to identify key factors
contributing to churn and predict which customers are most likely to leave.

**Goals & Outcomes:**
The primary goal is to provide insights that can help the company develop targeted retention
strategies. Key outcomes include a customer churn prediction model and visualizations of important
churn factors.

## Data

**Dataset:**

* **Source:** Kaggle (Telco Customer Churn dataset)
* **Description:** The dataset contains 7,043 rows and 21 columns, including customer
demographics, service usage, and account information. Key columns include: `gender`, `age`,
`contract`, `tenure`, `monthly_charges`, and `churn`.

## Tools and Methods

**Software and Libraries Used:**

* Python (Pandas, Matplotlib, Seaborn, scikit-learn)

**Analysis Approach:**
I used logistic regression to predict customer churn. The analysis involved data cleaning, feature
engineering, model training, and evaluation.

## Project Structure

---

**File Organization:**

  ├── data/
  │ └── telco_churn.csv
  ├── visualizations/
  │ ├── churn_rate_by_contract.png
  │ ├── feature_importance.png
  ├── code/
  │ └── churn_analysis.py
  └── README.md

## How to Understand/Reproduce the Analysis

**Steps to Understand/Reproduce the Analysis:**

1. **Data Loading:** The `telco_churn.csv` file was loaded into a Pandas DataFrame using
`pd.read_csv()`.
2. **Data Cleaning:** Missing values were handled, categorical variables were encoded, and
unnecessary columns were dropped.
3. **Feature Engineering:** New features such as `tenure_group` were created.
4. **Model Training:** A logistic regression model was trained on the prepared data using
`sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression`.
5. **Model Evaluation:** The model's performance was evaluated using metrics like accuracy,
precision, and recall.
6. **Visualizations:**
  * `churn_rate_by_contract.png`: A bar chart showing churn rate for different contract types,
created with Matplotlib.
  * `feature_importance.png`: A bar chart showing the importance of different features in the logistic
regression model, created with Seaborn.

## Key Findings and Insights

**Summary of Results:**

* Customers with month-to-month contracts are more likely to churn.
* Tenure is a significant factor, with shorter tenure associated with higher churn.
* Monthly charges also play a role in churn.

## Recommendations

**Actionable Insights:**

* Offer long-term contract discounts to incentivize customer retention.
* Develop targeted interventions for new customers to increase their engagement.
* Consider pricing strategies to address concerns about high monthly charges.

---

## Further Work

**Future Directions:**

* Explore other machine learning models, such as Random Forest or Gradient Boosting.
* Perform more detailed customer segmentation.
* Analyze customer feedback data to understand the reasons for churn in more detail.

Important Considerations When Choosing a Dataset:

Size and Complexity: For an introductory course, a smaller, well-structured dataset might be easier
to work with.

Variables Included: Ensure the dataset contains variables that will allow you to address your chosen
project idea.

Data Quality: Be aware that publicly available datasets may have missing values, inconsistencies, or
outliers that you'll need to handle.

Accessibility and Format: Check if the dataset is easy to download and in a format you can work with
(e.g., CSV, Excel).

Documentation: Look for any accompanying documentation that explains the variables and how the
data was collected.
