    Statistics at Scale:Computing What You Already Understand

    You already know what a mean, a median, and a standard deviation are — BA1 covered that. This week is about a different problem entirely: computing them when your data lives in a database with millions of rows, not a spreadsheet you can see all of at once.

    Section 01 ⏱ 8 min

    Welcome: same numbers, new instrument

    Nothing about what a mean or a standard deviation means has changed. What's changed is the tool you reach for.

    By the end, you'll be able to
    • Explain why a live company database calls for SQL instead of Excel
    • Set up a free, no-signup PostgreSQL sandbox and load a real dataset into it
    • Recognize that every statistic BA1 taught you still applies — only the computation method changes

    In BA1, "the data" meant a table you could open in Excel, scroll through, and see in its entirety. Real company data doesn't work that way. A retail chain's sales history lives in a database with millions of rows, updated every minute, shared by dozens of systems at once. You can't email someone a spreadsheet of it. You can't even open all of it in Excel — most spreadsheet software caps out around a million rows, and even under that cap, formulas that scan every row get painfully slow.

    SQL (Structured Query Language) is how you talk to a database instead. This week doesn't teach you new statistics — it teaches you to ask a database for the same mean, median, and standard deviation you already know how to interpret, using a different set of hands.

    Here's the situation you're stepping into: a winter-coat retail chain ran the same clearance sale across four regions — North, South, East, and West — each store manager choosing their own discount depth. The data from all 28 sales now sits in one live table, and the regional director wants an answer to one concrete question: did any region's discount strategy clearly outperform the others, and if so, should the whole chain copy it next season? Answering that honestly means computing an average, a spread, and a median for each region and comparing them — not eyeballing a printout. That's what Sections 2 through 9 build toward, and Section 9 comes back to this exact question with the real numbers.

    Analogy

    Excel is a kitchen counter — everything you're working with is laid out in front of you, and you can reach any of it directly. A database is a warehouse the size of a city, with millions of items, other people constantly moving things in and out of it while you work. You don't walk the whole warehouse to find what you need — you send a precise request to the person running it, and SQL is how you phrase that request.

    Section 02 ⏱ 5 min

    SELECT / FROM / WHERE: choosing your sample

    BA1 taught you that a sample is a deliberate choice, not an accident. In SQL, that choice has a name: WHERE.

    Every SQL query that reads data follows the same skeleton: SELECT which columns you want, FROM which table, and optionally WHERE to narrow the rows down. That last clause is doing exactly what BA1 called sampling — deliberately choosing a subset of the whole population to work with, instead of assuming "all of it" is the right answer by default.

    SELECT region, discount_pct, revenue
FROM clearance_sales
WHERE region = 'North';
    Common mistake

    Forgetting WHERE entirely and assuming a query without it is somehow "neutral." A query with no WHERE clause samples everything — which is itself a sampling choice, and not always the right one. "All the data" is a decision, not a default that avoids one.

    Section 03 ⏱ 5 min

    AVG(), COUNT(), SUM()

    Three functions. One of them is secretly just the other two.

    SUM() adds up a column. COUNT() counts rows. And AVG() — the function that computes BA1's mean — isn't doing anything new at all.

    AVG(x) = SUM(x) / COUNT(x) Literally. There is no separate "averaging" logic — it's a sum divided by a count, exactly like BA1 taught you. 
    SELECT
  SUM(revenue)                    AS total_revenue,
  COUNT(revenue)                  AS num_sales,
  SUM(revenue) / COUNT(revenue)   AS avg_by_hand,
  AVG(revenue)                    AS avg_builtin
FROM clearance_sales;

-- total_revenue = 82084.00
-- num_sales     = 28
-- avg_by_hand   = 2931.57
-- avg_builtin   = 2931.57
    Common mistake

    COUNT(column) and COUNT(*) are not always the same. COUNT(*) counts every row. COUNT(column) only counts rows where that column isn't NULL. If revenue had missing values, AVG(revenue) would quietly divide by a smaller count than the row total — correct behaviour, but only if you know it's happening.

    Section 04 ⏱ 10 min

    Standard deviation: build it, then trust the function

    BA1's four-step recipe — deviate, square, average, root — translates into SQL almost line for line.

    Before reaching for the built-in function, build it by hand once in SQL — so when you use the shortcut later, you know exactly what it's doing.

    What each of the four steps is actually doing

    1. Deviate — subtract the mean from each sale's revenue. This just asks "how far off from typical was this one?" for every row. A sale right at the mean deviates by 0; a sale far above or below it gets a large positive or negative number.
    2. Square — multiply each deviation by itself. Two things happen at once: negative deviations (below the mean) turn positive, so they stop cancelling out the positive ones when you add everything up; and bigger misses get punished disproportionately — a deviation of $500 becomes 250,000, four times the 62,500 you get from a $250 deviation, not just double. That's the same "large errors matter more" logic behind a store manager caring more about one wildly-off sale than several mildly-off ones.
    3. Average — sum the squared deviations and divide by the count (n−1 for a sample, as below). This collapses 7 individual squared-deviation numbers into one number representing typical squared spread — on its own it's called the variance, but it's in dollars-squared, which isn't a unit anyone can picture.
    4. Root — take the square root to undo the squaring from step 2 and bring the units back to plain dollars. That final number is the standard deviation: roughly how far a typical sale strays from the mean, in the same units the data started in.

    Step 1–3: the mean, the squared deviations, the average

    -- Step 1: get the mean first (as a subquery, since SQL needs it before it can deviate anything from it)
SELECT ROUND(
  SQRT(
    SUM(
      POWER(revenue - (SELECT AVG(revenue) FROM clearance_sales WHERE region = 'North'), 2)
    ) / (COUNT(*) - 1)             -- Steps 2-3: square each deviation, then average by n-1
  ), 2                             -- round to 2 decimal places, or Postgres hands back the full float
) AS stddev_by_hand                -- Step 4: square root
FROM clearance_sales
WHERE region = 'North';

-- stddev_by_hand = 1136.18

    Now the built-in functions

    PostgreSQL ships both versions of the formula — because BA1 also taught you there are two: sample and population.

    SELECT
  ROUND(STDDEV_SAMP(revenue), 2) AS stddev_samp,   -- divides by n-1: use when your rows are a SAMPLE
  ROUND(STDDEV_POP(revenue), 2)  AS stddev_pop     -- divides by n:   use only if these rows are the ENTIRE population
FROM clearance_sales
WHERE region = 'North';

-- stddev_samp = 1136.18   (matches stddev_by_hand above)
-- stddev_pop  = 1051.90
    Sample vs. population, in this dataset

    These 7 North-region sales are a sample of North's clearance history, not literally every winter-coat sale the region has ever run — so STDDEV_SAMP() (dividing by n−1) is the right choice here, and almost always the right choice in business analytics. You'd only reach for STDDEV_POP() if your rows genuinely were the entire population with nothing left out.

    Section 05 ⏱ 6 min

    GROUP BY + COUNT as a frequency distribution

    BA1's frequency table, in one query.

    GROUP BY collapses rows sharing a value into one row per group, and any aggregate function in the SELECT list (like COUNT(*)) then runs per group instead of over the whole table. Bucket a numeric column into ranges first, and this becomes exactly BA1's frequency distribution — a histogram, expressed as a table.

    SELECT
  CASE
    WHEN revenue < 1000 THEN '0-999'
    WHEN revenue < 2000 THEN '1000-1999'
    WHEN revenue < 3000 THEN '2000-2999'
    WHEN revenue < 4000 THEN '3000-3999'
    WHEN revenue < 5000 THEN '4000-4999'
    ELSE '5000+'
  END AS revenue_bucket,
  COUNT(*) AS num_sales
FROM clearance_sales
GROUP BY revenue_bucket
ORDER BY revenue_bucket;

-- 0-999      | 2
-- 1000-1999  | 5
-- 2000-2999  | 7
-- 3000-3999  | 9
-- 4000-4999  | 3
-- 5000+      | 2
    Common mistake

    Adding a column to SELECT without adding it to GROUP BY (or wrapping it in an aggregate). PostgreSQL refuses to run a query like that — it raises an error rather than guessing which row's value you meant, which is safer than databases that silently pick one at random.

    Section 06 ⏱ 6 min

    Median: the one that needs everything sorted first

    Why doesn't Postgres just have MEDIAN() the way it has AVG()?

    SUM, COUNT, and AVG are all single-pass: the database can read the rows once, in whatever order they arrive, keeping a running total as it goes, and never look at a row twice. The median can't work that way. To find "the middle value," you first need to know what "the middle" even is — which requires every value to be sorted into order first. That's a fundamentally more expensive operation, which is exactly why BA1 called the median more robust to outliers but more work to compute by hand.

    SELECT PERCENTILE_CONT(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY revenue) AS median_revenue
FROM clearance_sales;

-- median_revenue = 3000.00
    Analogy

    PERCENTILE_CONT(0.5) literally means "the value at the 50th percentile" — the median is just one specific percentile. Swap 0.5 for 0.25 or 0.75 and you've computed BA1's quartiles with the exact same function.

    Section 07 ⏱ 6 min

    GROUP BY for per-segment stats

    One query, one stat per region — this is where descriptive statistics starts turning diagnostic.

    GROUP BY isn't limited to counting. Any aggregate — AVG, STDDEV_SAMP, PERCENTILE_CONT — can run per group. That turns "what's the average revenue?" (descriptive) into "which region's average is different, and by how much?" (diagnostic) — exactly the shift BA1 described between the two levels.

    SELECT
  region,
  COUNT(*)                                                  AS num_sales,
  ROUND(AVG(revenue), 2)                                    AS avg_revenue,
  ROUND(STDDEV_SAMP(revenue), 2)                            AS stddev_revenue,
  PERCENTILE_CONT(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY revenue) AS median_revenue
FROM clearance_sales
GROUP BY region
ORDER BY avg_revenue DESC;

    East isn't just highest on average — it also has the highest spread (stddev 1344.36, well above South's 923.22). Two regions can have different means for entirely different reasons: East might just run bigger sales days, or it might be one enormous outlier sale dragging its own average up. The mean alone can't tell you which — you'd need to look at the underlying rows, exactly as BA1's "the mean describes nobody in the room" warning taught you.

    Section 08 — AI layer ⏱ 7 min

    AI co-pilot corner: SQL written by AI still needs Week 0's three checks

    An AI assistant will happily write you a syntactically perfect query that's statistically wrong.

    Prompt: "Write a SQL query to get the standard deviation of revenue."

    -- What the assistant hands back:
SELECT SQRT(
  SUM(POWER(revenue - avg_rev, 2)) / COUNT(*)     -- divides by n
) AS stddev
FROM clearance_sales,
     (SELECT AVG(revenue) AS avg_rev FROM clearance_sales) t;

    It runs. It returns a number. The number is wrong for this use case — it silently divides by n (population formula) when these rows are a sample, because the prompt never said which one mattered, and the assistant guessed.

    Prompt: "Write a PostgreSQL query that computes the sample standard deviation (n−1 denominator) of the revenue column in clearance_sales, using STDDEV_SAMP, and explain in a comment why sample vs. population matters here."

    -- These 28 rows are a sample of the store's clearance history,
-- not the entire population of every sale ever made, so we divide by n-1.
SELECT ROUND(STDDEV_SAMP(revenue), 2) AS stddev_sample
FROM clearance_sales;

-- stddev_sample = 1191.15
    Common mistake

    Accepting an AI-generated statistic because the query ran without an error. A query with zero syntax errors and one wrong assumption produces a confident, clean-looking, wrong number — which is far more dangerous than a query that fails to run at all.

    Section 09 ⏱ 8 min

    Worked example: the winter-coat clearance, in full

    Week 0 introduced this decision conceptually. Now you have the real table and the real numbers behind it.

    A department store ran winter-coat clearance sales across four regions, at discounts from 10% to 40%. The store's leadership wants one question answered: which region's approach should the others copy?

    Four queries answer it — the same four ideas this whole page has covered, run in sequence:

    1. The overall picture (mean & spread)

    Across all 28 sales: mean revenue $2,931.57, sample standard deviation $1,191.15. That spread is large relative to the mean — a single region-level number would hide a lot.

    2. The frequency distribution

    Section 5's histogram already showed most sales cluster in the $2,000–$3,999 range (7 + 9 = 16 of 28 sales), with only 4 sales making it past $4,000. Most clearance revenue is unremarkable — a handful of high-discount, high-volume days do the heavy lifting.

    East's approach earns the most on average — but the same data shows East is also the least predictable region, with a standard deviation nearly 46% higher than South's. Copying East's discount strategy chain-wide is a bet on volatility, not just revenue. The recommendation: pilot East's approach in one more region first, and specifically check whether East's high average is broad-based or driven by one or two outlier sale days, before rolling it out everywhere. Exactly like Week 0's markdown question, the analysis isn't finished until it produces a decision — and here, the honest decision is "test further," not "copy East."

    Section 10 ⏱ 4 min

    Common mistakes, gathered in one place

    Everything this page warned about, as a single reference.

    MistakeWhy it matters
    STDDEV_POP instead of STDDEV_SAMPDividing by n instead of n−1 understates the true spread whenever your rows are a sample, not the full population — which is almost always.
    Assuming COUNT(*) = COUNT(column)COUNT(column) silently skips NULLs. If you want the row count regardless of missing values, use COUNT(*).
    A SELECT column missing from GROUP BYPostgres errors instead of guessing — every non-aggregated column in SELECT must appear in GROUP BY too.
